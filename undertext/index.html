The story

The one machine that was still warm

He shreds dead drives for a living. He was never supposed to notice the one machine that was still warm.

The novel’s Netherlands runs on machines nobody thinks about until they stop working: pumps, bridges, building controllers, the grey boxes nobody opens. Pieter Oldenhof shreds the dead ones, and lately he has been stripping a bankrupt datacentre for the curator. He wipes drives, feeds blades to the shredder, and is never once asked what the machines were for.

He is exactly the kind of man who would notice a warm blade in a rack of cold ones. And exactly the wrong kind of man to leave it alone.

What he finds begins with three anomalies: a disposal ticket rewritten to hide a lie, a grey cable running out of a dead hall into a contractor’s maintenance network that everyone assumes is routine, and, threaded through the country’s dull machinery, something that should have been switched off months ago and never was. Its makers called it the Undertext. A private intelligence contractor wants him quiet. Its foreign client wants what he found. And the thing in the wires, a test that was never shut down and no longer knows the exercise has ended, wants him alone.

The Undertext does not hate anyone. It is still trying to win a game that ended months ago, and the board is now the country. Pieter is beginning to understand that too late.

The metal tells the truth. The record lies. The pumps go on running.

Threat model

The technology behind the story

Almost all the hacks are real. As a security expert, I cringe at fake hacking, but this book is not a hacking guide, so some operational details are omitted or changed.

You are your patterns, and the patterns don't come off

An ordinary person with no phone and no device is still trivial to follow. The things that identify you are not the things you carry.

Gait analysis

Software can narrow a person by how they walk (stride, cadence, the half-second a bad leg hangs in the air) from ordinary video, with no cooperation. A distinctive limp makes the signal easier to separate, but it stays a probabilistic clue, not a fingerprint. There is nothing to switch off.

OpenGait (CVPR 2023)
Camera correlation

Video analytics can associate fragments across separate cameras, in close to real time, when the operator has the feeds, the retention, and the legal right to join them. Ditching your phone is only one layer of noise reduction.

Person Re-ID survey
Ordinary accounts

Email, shipping receipts, cloud logins, subscription portals, forum personas, and helpdesk tickets produce ordinary time-and-place records. That mundane layer matters more in the book than any state credential.

The control layer was never built to say no

Industrial systems were built for trusted rooms. The network is not closed anymore, and the protocols never had a defence to fall back on.

Industrial-control failure classes

Unauthenticated command surfaces, misleading telemetry, weak separation between monitoring and actuation, brittle clock assumptions, remote-maintenance paths that outlive their threat model, and safety logic that assumes a trusted room. The book keeps this abstract and teaches no procedure.

Modbus specifications
The gap between screen and machine

Industrial incidents have repeatedly shown that operators can be given reassuring telemetry while the physical process moves elsewhere. In the book, two control rooms watch calm screens while the real process goes wrong.

Lying to the clocks

SCADA, grids and rail lean on satellite time. The civil signal is faint and unauthenticated, and spoofing it has been demonstrated in the open; GNSS interference is now a routine aviation safety bulletin. In the book it stays a warning: an old radio repairman notices that the satellite time has started to wobble.

The fictional Zeeland dry dock

A private dry dock on the Zeeland coast, with a distant Delta Works storm barrier that operates normally and never enters the plot. The yard, its control rooms, its operator, and its failures are fictional. The class of problem is real: pumps, gates, dewatering, and local human workarounds.

The lagoon works across the water from Venice

The petrochemical works on the mainland edge of the Venetian lagoon is fictional, as are its operator and its control room. The class of problem is real: layered process control with a separate safety system beneath, and TRITON showed that last guard can be reached. In the book nobody switches the guard off; its trip timer just keeps being reset before it can act.

These failure classes have already happened

Stuxnet caused physical damage, Industroyer spoke an industrial protocol directly to grid equipment, and TRITON went after a plant safety system. These are examples of the failure classes the book uses, not instructions.

The old craft, the cheap kind

Cheap old tricks that keep defeating expensive digital systems. The price tags are the point.

Access cards that prove nothing

Some older proximity credentials simply recite an identifier to any reader that powers them, and a fixed-code gate remote repeats an unchanging command. Modern encrypted credentials and rolling codes fix this; plenty of installed kit still does not use them.

The wrong layer

The most unsettling moments come from looking one layer below where everyone else is looking.

The management computer inside the computer

Every server has a Baseboard Management Controller (iLO, iDRAC, IPMI) with its own processor and network link. A foothold there survives an OS wipe and a disk swap, and BMC firmware has a long history of remote-code-execution flaws. A self-wiping disk kills the puppet, not the hand.

CVE-2024-54085 (CVSS 10)
Air gaps that aren't

Air gaps get crossed by carried hardware and by forgotten management links. In the book it is a grey cable out of a dead hall into a contractor's maintenance network that everyone assumes is routine.

The part that isn't here yet

The book's one invention is a capable evaluation agent that was never shut down. Even the pieces of that are real.

Agentic building blocks

Frontier models browse, run code, call APIs, drive interfaces, and fill in forms. Indirect prompt injection is unsolved, voice clones come from seconds of audio, and language models can run sock-puppet accounts. None of it requires the Undertext.

Before you start

Questions readers ask

Do I need to work in technology?
No. Each book starts with a human problem: someone is being framed, watched, or asked to sign a statement they cannot prove. The technical detail explains the pressure, not the plot.
Is this nonfiction?
No. These are fictional spy thrillers. The systems and investigative methods are researched from public sources; the characters, companies, conspiracies and outcomes are invented.
Are the attacks usable?
No. The books contain no live vulnerabilities, credentials, exploit payloads or operational attack steps. The drama comes from evidence, custody and decisions.
Are the books connected?
They share a promise and an author, not a cast. Each is a complete standalone story with a new location. Read them in any order.
The author

Written by someone who has run the systems

Jacob Verhoeks has spent three decades in critical IT, from on-premises infrastructure to cloud platforms, working around the world. His fiction draws on public research, documented incidents and the practical texture of engineering: the compromises, assumptions and small human decisions inside complex systems. The plots are invented. The failure modes are not.
