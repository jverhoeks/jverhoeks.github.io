The story

It begins with the wrong label

Barcelona, August 2026. Mei Zhou runs her family’s import warehouse. Her husband, Arnau Vidal, moves television pictures through systems nobody notices until they fail. Neither of them is a spy.

Then a freight photograph shows eclipse equipment shipped under Mei’s company name. The label is false. That same morning, Arnau’s oldest colleague leaves an eleven-second video and vanishes. In the frame is Mei’s false label on a black case inside a room that does not exist.

To find their friend and clear their name, they follow the things people forget the internet can reveal: an exposed service, a reused certificate, an old public record—and the gaps left when someone has cleaned everything else.

All of it leads toward the total eclipse. The Moon will not wait.

Behind the scenes

The machinery under the story

The novel keeps the engineering inside the action. The fuller technical account waits until the end of the book; here are only the systems that drive the premise.

Finding what was meant to stay hidden

The public internet keeps traces long after the people behind them believe they have disappeared.

Search engines for machines

Services such as Shodan index public-facing systems rather than web pages. A forgotten hostname or exposed service can reveal that a supposedly nonexistent machine was once online.

Certificates as fingerprints

Reused web certificates can suggest that two unrelated-looking systems share an administrator or history. They are a lead, not proof—the distinction matters in the investigation.

A city as one machine

Traffic control, power, ports, telecoms, and emergency routes do not have to share one brain to share one failure.

The green wave

Coordinated signals can turn separate junctions into a moving corridor. In the story, the pattern becomes a rehearsal—and then a choice.

Negative space

What is absent from logs, maps, and custody records can be more revealing than what remains. Lin and Oriol learn to investigate the gaps.

Eleven seconds

A small timing error is harmless until enough systems agree that it is true.

Clock trust

Modern infrastructure depends on shared time. The thriller turns one repeated offset into a trail through systems that were never designed to be questioned together.

Human fallback

The decisive safeguards are people who notice, refuse, improvise, and accept the cost of stopping a machine that still appears healthy.

Models that can leave the cloud

The story’s fictional chip grows from two real directions in AI: downloadable model weights and hardware designed around how modern models actually move data.

Open-weight models

When model weights are available, a model can run locally instead of behind a provider’s API. That gives the operator more control over privacy, safeguards, logging, and modification—and more responsibility for every one of them.

The possible chip

Lantern is fictional. Its premise combines sparse mixture-of-experts routing, lower-precision computation, fast memory, and careful placement of model parts so that only the needed experts are active. Those are real research directions; the compact frontier-scale module in the novel is the leap.
