You can't take your patterns off

How you follow a man with no phone and no device.

Real Gait analysis

Software identifies you by how you walk (stride, cadence, the hitch of a bad leg) from ordinary CCTV, with no cooperation. A limp makes the signature stronger, not weaker. There is nothing to switch off.

OpenGait (CVPR 2023)
Real Camera correlation

Modern video analytics track one person across a whole city's cameras in near real-time, stitching the feeds into a single trail. Ditching your phone barely helps.

Person Re-ID survey
Real The smart meter as a beacon

Dutch smart meters report usage every 15 minutes, enough to know you're home and just boiled a kettle. The same module can be told to open the relay and cut your power.

DSMR P1 standard
Real DigiD & the BSN

One national identity keys your entire official existence. You can't see a doctor or file a form without touching it, and touching it announces exactly where and when.

Logius DigiD
Real The 2G trick, and its trap

Thirty-year-old broken GSM crypto lets a few euros of radio impersonate another subscriber. But as 2G is switched off (NL: end of 2027), lighting up a dead band makes you more conspicuous, not less.

The control layer was never built to say no

The systems that run water and power were made for trusted rooms that no longer exist.

Real Protocols with no password

Modbus and IEC 60870-5-104, still running water and power, have no authentication by design. The equipment obeys whoever speaks last. The Dutch Court of Audit confirmed in 2019 that an attacker could take over the vital water works.

Rekenkamer report (2019)
Real The lie between screen and world

The quiet attack is not sabotage. Idle a pump, hold a gate, and feed the control room the readings it expects. The screen shows a calm green normal while the water actually climbs. No fault, nothing broke.

Real Lying to the clocks

SCADA, grids and rail lean on GPS time. The signal is faint and unauthenticated; a few hundred dollars spoofs it, and protection logic trips itself off for a phase error real in every measurement and false in every cause.

TEXBAT spoofing tests
Real Porto Marghera, a mile from Venice

Porto Marghera's live process units sit across a mile of open lagoon from Venice, run on layered computer control with a separate safety system beneath. TRITON proved that last guard can be reached.

Real It already happened

None of this is theory. Stuxnet physically wrecked Iranian centrifuges. Industroyer spoke IEC 60870-5-104 straight to a power grid. In late 2023, attackers took over US water-authority PLCs through default passwords.

CISA: water-authority PLC takeover, 2023

Thirty euros of radio

Cheap analogue tools that keep beating expensive digital systems. The price tags are the point.

Real Software-defined radio

A EUR 25 USB stick turns any laptop into a wideband radio receiver. Sold as a hobbyist toy, and able to pull a subscriber identity out of the air at a tram stop.

RTL-SDR
Real Access cards that prove nothing

Low-end proximity cards just recite their number to any reader, copyable through a coat for the price of a coffee. Fixed-code gate remotes replay the same packet forever.

Proxmark3 / OpenSesame

The wrong layer

The most unsettling moments come from looking one layer below where everyone else is looking.

Real The computer inside the computer

Every server has a second computer on the motherboard (iLO, iDRAC, IPMI) that runs with the host switched off and survives an OS wipe and a disk swap, with a long history of remote-code-execution holes. A self-wiping disk kills the puppet, not the hand.

CVE-2024-54085 (CVSS 10)
Real The analogue never went dark

Air gaps get crossed by carried hardware and forgotten management links. And a device's current draw leaks what its processor is doing, readable from mains-socket distance, on a layer nobody was listening to.

Read the opening

On the evening of Saturday, 27 June 2026, the country that had spent a thousand years learning to watch water briefly lost the network it used to watch.

It began, the notice would later say, during planned work on a fibre-optic cable. A fault left Rijkswaterstaat without internet access across the country. Systems used for bridges, locks, tunnels, motorway signs, cameras, and public information were disrupted. Where they could, operators switched to local control.

It happened, as these things do, on the worst possible afternoon. A line of summer storms was crossing the country as the systems failed, hail and standing water and a wind that put branches across the A-roads, and for some hours the Netherlands managed its weather as it had before any of the machines existed: by guess, by telephone, and by the older habit of people telling one another what they could see out of their own windows.

But consider, only consider, the shape of it. For a few hours on a stormy Saturday the most thoroughly watched stretch of road in Europe could not see all of itself. The country that photographs every number plate on every motorway, that had built its safety out of never once looking away, that country, officially, lost part of its sight.

And a blind man is not the only kind of man in a dark room. He is only the one who cannot see the others.

Nobody could prove that anything used those hours. There was no evidence. There is never evidence; that is the nature of the thing. There was only, afterward, for anyone who knew where to stand and how to look, the smallest wrongness left in the record, the kind an engineer notices and a spokesman does not.

What had moved in the blindness waited, patient, through the warm forgettable weeks and into the rain and dark of November, and what it did then is the rest of this book.
